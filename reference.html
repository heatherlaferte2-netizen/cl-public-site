Accountable Talk Moves

Use during discussion. The goal is to build, not perform.

Building On "I want to build on what [name] said — I think the same thing is happening in..."
Agreeing + Evidence "I agree with [name], and the evidence I'd add is..."
Respectful Challenge "I see it differently — I think the text is actually saying..."
Pressing for Reasoning "What makes you say that?" / "Can you say more about why?"
Clarifying "Can you clarify what you mean by ___? I want to make sure I understand."
Returning to Text "I think the text says something different — in [line/passage]..."
Synthesizing "It sounds like we're saying two things: [A] and [B]. Are those compatible?"

Thesis, Claims & Evidence Integration

Writing

The Claim Formula

A thesis is a specific, arguable claim — not a fact, not a topic, not a question. It commits you to something someone could disagree with.

Literary Analysis

In [title], [author] [argues / reveals / demonstrates] that [specific arguable claim about theme, character, or craft], as evidenced by [brief indication of what you'll examine].

Argumentative

[Specific position] because [reason 1], [reason 2], and despite [acknowledgment of complexity or counterargument].

Explanatory / Informational

[Subject] can be understood through [organizing principle or framework], specifically [the aspects you will explain].

Thesis Test — Ask Yourself:

  • Could a thoughtful reader disagree? (If not, it's a fact, not a claim.)
  • Is it specific enough that I know exactly what I have to prove?
  • Does it go beyond restating the prompt?

The Evidence-Analysis Move

Evidence without analysis is quotation. Analysis without evidence is assertion. You need both, connected.

The Three-Step Move

  1. Introduce. Signal phrase + citation context
  2. Evidence. The quote, paraphrase, or example
  3. Analyze. "This reveals / suggests / demonstrates..." + the so what

Signal Phrases

argues that
reveals
demonstrates
suggests
illustrates
establishes
acknowledges
contends
complicates
challenges

The Analysis Must Answer:

  • "What does this evidence reveal about [character / theme / argument]?"
  • "Why does this word choice / structure / choice matter?"
  • "So what? Why does this evidence support my claim specifically?"

If your "analysis" just restates the quote in different words, you haven't analyzed. Ask "so what?" again.

Sentence Diagramming Quick Reference & MLA Basics

Language · Research

Sentence Diagram — Reed-Kellogg Symbols

The diagram shows how parts of a sentence relate. Always ask: what does this structure accomplish?

Basic Structure

subject | predicate verb | \ predicate adjective/noun

Modifiers

modifier ___________ subject | predicate

Adjectives and adverbs branch diagonally below the word they modify.

Prepositional Phrase

subject | verb \ prep. \ object

Relative / Dependent Clause

subject | predicate [relative clause branches on a pedestal below the noun it modifies]

Compound Elements

subject | verb \ / conj. (dotted line joins / \ parallel elements)

The Essential Question

After every diagram: What does this structure do? Why did the writer build the sentence this way?

MLA Citation Basics

In-text: (Author page#). Works Cited: alphabetical by author last name, hanging indent. When in doubt, cite.

Source TypeWorks Cited Format
Book Author Last, First. Title of Book. Publisher, Year.
Article / Essay (in print) Author Last, First. "Title of Article." Title of Magazine/Journal, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. ##–##.
Website / Online Article Author Last, First. "Title of Page." Site Name, Date of Publication, URL.
Article from Database Author Last, First. "Title." Journal, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. ##–##. Database Name, DOI or URL.

In-Text Citation Rules

  • After a quote or paraphrase: (Smith 42)
  • No page numbers (website): (Smith)
  • Author named in signal phrase: just (42)
  • Period goes after the citation, not before: "...the truth" (Smith 14).

Quoting vs. Paraphrasing

  • Quote when exact wording matters — cite and put in quotation marks
  • Paraphrase when the idea matters more than the exact wording — cite, no quotation marks, genuinely rewrite in your own words (not just swap synonyms)
  • Summary when you need the overall point of a whole source — cite

When in doubt about whether to cite: cite. It is always safer to over-attribute than under-attribute.

Literary & Rhetorical Terms — A through M

Reading · Speaking

Allusion

A reference to another text, event, person, or cultural work that imports that work's meaning into the current text.

Anachronism

Something placed in a time period where it doesn't belong — in literature, often deliberate and meaningful.

Anaphora

Repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses for emphasis and rhythm.

Antagonist

The force or character in opposition to the protagonist — not necessarily a villain; can be a circumstance, society, or internal conflict.

Chiasmus

A rhetorical figure where the second half of a statement mirrors the first in reverse order. "Not everything that is faced can be changed, but nothing can be changed until it is faced." (Baldwin)

Connotation

The emotional, cultural, or associative meanings a word carries beyond its literal definition. Contrast with denotation.

Denotation

The literal, dictionary definition of a word — what it means stripped of emotional association.

Dramatic Irony

When the audience knows something a character doesn't, creating tension or tragic effect.

Ethos

Rhetorical appeal based on the speaker's credibility, authority, or character. One of Aristotle's three modes of persuasion.

Euphemism

A mild or indirect expression used in place of a blunt or harsh one. Often obscures reality — worth analyzing what is being softened and why.

Fallacy

A flaw in reasoning — an argument that appears valid but isn't. Common types: ad hominem, straw man, false dichotomy, slippery slope, hasty generalization.

Foil

A character who contrasts with another character to highlight particular traits. The foil doesn't have to be an antagonist.

Hyperbole

Deliberate exaggeration for effect. Signals emotional intensity — worth asking what the exaggeration reveals about the speaker.

Imagery

Language that appeals to the senses — sight, sound, taste, touch, smell. Effective imagery creates embodied understanding, not just visual pictures.

Intertextuality

The way texts reference, respond to, or build upon other texts. All literature exists in conversation with what came before it.

Logos

Rhetorical appeal based on logic, evidence, and reasoning. Can be misused through fallacious logic or cherry-picked data.

Metaphor

A direct comparison that transfers meaning from one thing to another without using "like" or "as." Metaphors frame how we understand their subjects — what they include and what they exclude matters.

Metonymy

A figure of speech in which a related concept substitutes for the intended concept. "The pen is mightier than the sword."

Motif

A recurring element — image, phrase, object, situation — that develops meaning through repetition. Distinct from theme: a motif is a recurring element; a theme is the idea the work develops.

Oxymoron

Two contradictory terms combined — "deafening silence," "bittersweet." Holds a tension that a single word cannot.

Literary & Rhetorical Terms — N through Z

Reading · Speaking

Narrator

The voice telling the story. Not the author. First person (I), second person (you), third limited (he/she/they, limited to one character's perspective), third omniscient (access to all characters).

Pathos

Rhetorical appeal based on emotion, values, or empathy. Not inherently manipulative — connecting to shared human experience is legitimate rhetoric.

Personification

Giving human qualities to non-human things. Ask: what kind of person is this non-human thing being compared to? What does that imply?

Protagonist

The central character whose journey or conflict drives the narrative. Not necessarily the hero or a morally good character.

Register

The variety of language appropriate to a particular social situation or purpose. Formal, informal, academic, colloquial — you code-switch between registers constantly.

Rhetoric

The art of effective communication — specifically, the study of how language persuades, informs, and moves audiences. Not a synonym for "empty speech."

Satire

The use of humor, irony, or exaggeration to critique human behavior, society, or institutions. Usually has a serious underlying purpose.

Simile

A comparison using "like" or "as." Unlike metaphor, simile maintains a clear boundary between the two things being compared.

Subtext

What is implied rather than stated — the meaning beneath the surface of what characters say or do. Often more significant than the text itself.

Synecdoche

A figure of speech in which a part stands for the whole (or vice versa). "All hands on deck." Ask: what does the choice of part reveal about the whole?

Syntax

The arrangement of words and phrases to create sentences. Syntax is a craft choice — how a sentence is built shapes how it feels and what it means.

Theme

An arguable claim about human experience that a text develops — not a topic word ("love," "war") but a statement about what the text argues about that topic. Must be more specific than "the theme is loyalty."

Tone

The author's attitude toward the subject, expressed through language. Lives in diction, syntax, what is included and omitted. Distinct from mood (the reader's emotional response).

Warrant

The underlying assumption that connects evidence to a claim in an argument. Often unstated — making warrants explicit reveals whether an argument's logic actually holds.

AR 2023 ELA Standards — What They Mean for You

All Strands · Grades 9–10

These are the skills this course develops. The codes (RL.9-10.1, etc.) appear on rubrics and feedback. Here's what they mean in plain language.

StandardIn Plain Language
Reading: Literary Text
RL.9-10.1I can cite the best available evidence from a literary text and explain what the text implies beyond what it states directly.
RL.9-10.2I can identify what a literary text argues about human experience (theme) and trace how that argument develops across the text.
RL.9-10.3I can analyze how complex characters develop, interact, and drive plot and theme — especially when they have conflicting motivations.
RL.9-10.4I can analyze how specific word choices — including figurative language — create cumulative effects on meaning and tone.
RL.9-10.5I can analyze how an author's structural choices — order, pacing, parallel plots, time manipulation — create effects like tension or surprise.
RL.9-10.6I can analyze how a particular cultural experience or perspective shapes what a literary text is able to say.
RL.9-10.7I can compare how the same subject is represented in two different artistic mediums — what each emphasizes and what each leaves out.
RL.9-10.9I can analyze how an author draws on and transforms source material (myth, earlier literature, history) in their own work.
Reading: Informational Text
RI.9-10.1I can cite strong evidence from informational texts and recognize what they imply beyond what they state directly.
RI.9-10.2I can identify the central idea of an informational text and trace how it develops across the whole piece.
RI.9-10.6I can identify an author's point of view and purpose, and analyze how they use rhetoric (ethos, logos, pathos) to advance it.
RI.9-10.7I can integrate and evaluate information from multiple sources, recognizing what each emphasizes and omits.
RI.9-10.8I can evaluate whether an argument's reasoning is valid and its evidence is relevant and sufficient — and identify fallacies.
RI.9-10.9I can analyze how seminal U.S. documents address enduring themes and questions.
Writing
W.9-10.1I can write an argument with a precise claim, valid reasoning, relevant and sufficient evidence, and fair treatment of counterarguments.
W.9-10.2I can write explanatory texts that convey complex ideas clearly through effective organization, precise language, and appropriate examples.
W.9-10.3I can write narratives using effective technique, well-chosen details, pacing, dialogue, and meaningful reflection.
W.9-10.5I can develop my writing through planning, revising, editing, and seeking feedback — not just turning in a first draft.
W.9-10.9I can draw evidence from literary and informational texts to support my analysis, not just as decoration.

SL.9-10.1I can participate in academic discussion — building on others' ideas, citing evidence, asking for reasoning, and contributing my own thinking.

Language

L.9-10.4I can determine the meaning of unfamiliar words using context clues, morphology, and reference materials.
L.9-10.5I can understand and use figurative language, word relationships, and nuances in word meaning with precision.
L.9-10.6I can use academic and domain-specific vocabulary accurately in reading, writing, speaking, and listening.
