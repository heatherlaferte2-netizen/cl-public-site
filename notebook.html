Tool 1 · Composition Book

The Private Journal

Stays in the classroom · Write in it every day

This is your personal writing space. You write in it at the start and close of every period. It is never collected, never graded for content, and never read without your permission — including by the teacher.

It holds your free writes, your reactions to what we read, your word-of-the-year work, and whatever else is on your mind. The composition book is yours in the truest sense.

The Composition Journal

Your Private Writing Space

The One Rule That Does Not Change

No one reads your journal without your permission.

Not the teacher. Not another student. Not a substitute. This journal is yours. That means you can write honestly in it. You can be confused in it. You can be angry or excited or uncertain in it. Nothing you write in it will be held against you or used to grade you. The only thing that counts is that you actually write. What you write is between you and the page.

Start of every period

Opening Write

The journal opens with you. Before any lesson, before any instruction, you pick up your journal from the shelf and write. The prompt may be on the board, or it may be open — write what is on your mind. Three to five minutes. Every period. No exceptions.

End of every period

Closing Write

Before the bell, the journal comes back out. One sentence to one paragraph: what happened in your thinking today? What are you still wondering? What changed? This is how you know you learned something. Return the journal to the shelf on your way out.

First week of school

Word of the Year

The first week in your journal is dedicated to finding your word for the year. Not assigned — discovered. The journal is where you do that searching, day by day, until the word finds you.

All year long

What Else Lives Here

Free writes prompted by what we read. Reactions to class discussions. Drafts of ideas before they become academic work. Questions you don't want to ask out loud. The things you need to say before you know how to say them in an essay.

First Week Ritual

Word of the Year

You do not choose your word on the first day. You spend the first week discovering it. The journal is where that discovery happens — one day at a time, until the right word rises to the surface. At the end of the week, you commit.

Day 1 · Thu
The teacher shares hers first. You listen. You wonder. What would your word be? No pressure to decide. Just begin to notice.
Day 2 · Fri
First journal entry around the idea. Three questions on the board. You write to them in your journal for ten minutes. No sharing required. Just honest thinking on paper.
Day 3 · Mon
What keeps coming up? Look at what you wrote Friday. What words appeared more than once? What ideas kept circling? Write more. You are narrowing, not choosing.
Day 4 · Tue
From many to two or three. Write your candidate words at the top of a new page. Under each one: one sentence about why it matters to you right now. Which one makes you feel something when you read it?
Day 5 · Wed
Commitment day. You choose your word. You decorate an index card with it — the design is yours. The card goes on the classroom bulletin board, where it will live all year. Your word stays visible every day you are in this room.
BRAVE

Your design · Your handwriting · Your word

The index card is yours to design. Some students draw. Some use color. Some write their word large and leave the rest plain. What matters is that the card is genuinely yours — not decorated to impress anyone.

Once the cards go on the bulletin board, they stay there. You will walk past your word every day. At the end of each unit, you will return to it in your journal: how has your relationship to this word changed?

This is your year. This is your word. Choose it like it matters — because it does.

The Academic Binder

Four Tabs · Organized Academic Work

The binder is your academic home base. It holds everything that is not private — the close reading work, the argument analysis, the writing drafts, the handouts, and the reference materials. It is organized with four labeled dividers.

1
Class Notes & Daily Work

Everything from in-class instruction. Annotations, exit tickets, close reading notes, SOAPStone charts, observation vs. inference charts. Date-stamped. Chronological.

  • Annotations from class reading
  • Exit tickets
  • In-class charts and graphic organizers
2
Reading & Analysis

Text-specific academic work. SOAPStone completed forms, argument tracking, rhetorical move identification, evidence collection organized by text.

  • Completed SOAPStone forms
  • Evidence and inference charts
  • Rhetorical move trackers
3
Writing & Drafts

Analytical writing from first attempt through final draft. Topic sentences, evidence paragraphs, essay drafts, peer response notes, and teacher feedback.

  • Analytical paragraph drafts
  • Essay outlines and full drafts
  • Feedback and revision notes
4
Reference Materials

Tools you return to across the year. Grammar and sentence guides, literary terms, SOAPStone reference card, rhetorical strategies glossary, vocabulary by unit.

  • Grammar and diagramming reference
  • Literary terms and rhetorical devices
  • Vocabulary by unit
Handout policy: All handouts are hole-punched and filed in the relevant binder tab — not floating loose, not left in the classroom, not folded in a pocket. When you receive a handout, the first thing you do is hole-punch it and file it. Your binder is only useful if it is actually organized. If you do not have a hole punch, the teacher keeps one at the front of the room.

Where Your Journal Lives & How You Get It

Journals stay in the classroom. This protects them. This also means there is a system for getting yours at the start of every period and returning it at the end. The system is simple and runs itself after the first week.

The Daily Routine

Enter Pick up your journal from the shelf on the way to your seat. This happens before you sit down — it is part of the motion of arriving.
Open Journal opens before instruction begins. Prompt may be on the board or open free write. Begin writing. The teacher takes attendance while you settle into writing.
During Journal stays at the corner of your desk. Available for closing write and any mid-class personal reflection moments.
Close Closing write near the end of class. One sentence to one paragraph. The teacher ends the period — not the bell.
Exit Return your journal to the shelf on your way out. Same spot, same bin, every time. If it is not back on the shelf, the system breaks for everyone.

For Students & Families

Questions We Hear

  • If the journal is private, how is it graded?

    The journal is graded for use only — not for content. The teacher can see that pages are being written on without reading what is written. At the end of each quarter, students complete a brief self-assessment reflecting on their own journal habits. What you write is private. Whether you wrote is part of the daily grade.

  • Can I take my journal home?

    Journals stay in the classroom. This is a school preference and a practical one — journals that go home tend to get lost, left behind, or not brought back. Keeping them in the classroom means they are always available, always protected, and never a homework crisis.

  • What if I want to share something from my journal?

    Sharing is always voluntary. Students are invited to share passages during class at various points in the year — but the invitation is never a requirement. The teacher will never ask to read a student's journal without the student offering. Some students choose to show the teacher a passage. Some students never share a word. Both are completely fine.

  • What is the binder for and does it come home?

    The binder is the academic notebook — class notes, reading analysis, writing drafts, and reference materials. The binder stays in the classroom on its own labeled shelf spot. The school prefers binders stay at school so they don't get lost or left behind — students study from it during class and any independent work time, not as homework that requires bringing it home.

  • What is the word of the year?

    During the first week of school, each student chooses one word that will guide their year — a word they want to grow into, move toward, or live by. Students spend the week discovering it through journal writing rather than choosing it on the spot. The word goes on a decorated index card that lives on the classroom bulletin board all year. At the end of each unit, students return to it in their journal and reflect on how their relationship to the word has changed.
