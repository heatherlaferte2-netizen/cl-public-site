In discussion: build on each other, cite evidence, press for reasoning.

Clarify"Can you clarify what you mean by ___? I want to understand."
Return to Text"The text says something different — in [line/passage]..."
Synthesize"It sounds like we're arguing [A] and [B]. Are those compatible?"

Thesis, Claims & Evidence Integration

Writing

The Claim Formula

A thesis is specific and arguable — not a fact, not a topic, not a question. It commits you to something a thoughtful reader could disagree with.

Literary Analysis

In [title], [author] [argues / reveals / demonstrates] that [specific arguable claim], as evidenced by [what you'll examine].

Argumentative

[Specific position] because [reason 1] and [reason 2], despite [acknowledgment of complexity].

Explanatory

[Subject] can be understood through [organizing principle], specifically [the aspects you will explain].

Thesis Test — Ask Yourself:

  • Could a thoughtful reader disagree? (If not: it's a fact, not a claim.)
  • Is it specific enough that I know exactly what to prove?
  • Does it go beyond restating the prompt?

The Evidence-Analysis Move

Evidence without analysis is quotation. Analysis without evidence is assertion. You need both, connected.

The Three-Step Move

  1. Introduce — signal phrase + context
  2. Evidence — quote, paraphrase, or example
  3. Analyze — "This reveals / suggests..." + the so what

Signal Phrases

argues that
reveals
demonstrates
suggests
illustrates
establishes
complicates
challenges

The Analysis Must Answer:

  • "What does this reveal about [theme / character / argument]?"
  • "Why does this word choice / structure matter here?"
  • "So what? Why does this support my specific claim?"

If your analysis restates the quote, ask "so what?" again.

Sentence Diagramming Quick Reference & MLA Basics

Language · Research

Reed-Kellogg Sentence Diagrams

The diagram shows how parts relate. Always ask: what does this structure accomplish?

Basic Structure

subject | predicate verb \ predicate adjective / noun

Modifiers (branch diagonally below)

adj./adv. ___________ subject | predicate

Prepositional Phrase

subject | verb \ prep. \ object

Dependent / Relative Clause

subject | predicate [clause on pedestal below the noun it modifies]

Compound / Parallel Elements

elem. \ conj. ← dotted line joins / parallel elements elem.

After every diagram: Why did the writer build it this way?

MLA Citation Basics

In-text: (Author page). Works Cited: alphabetical by last name, hanging indent. When in doubt, cite.

SourceWorks Cited Format
BookLast, First. Title. Publisher, Year.
Print ArticleLast, First. "Title." Journal, vol. #, no. #, Year, pp. #–#.
WebsiteLast, First. "Page Title." Site Name, Date, URL.
DatabaseLast, First. "Title." Journal, vol. #, Year, pp. #–#. Database, DOI or URL.

In-Text Rules

  • After quote or paraphrase: (Smith 42)
  • No page numbers (website): (Smith)
  • Author named in sentence: just (42)
  • Period goes after the citation: "...truth" (Smith 14).

Quote vs. Paraphrase

  • Quote when exact wording matters — cite, use quotation marks
  • Paraphrase when the idea matters more — cite, genuinely rewrite
  • Summary for the point of a whole source — cite

Literary & Rhetorical Terms — A through M

Reading · Speaking

Allusion

A reference to another text, event, or cultural work that imports that work's meaning into the current text.

Anachronism

Something placed in a time period where it doesn't belong — in literature, often deliberate and meaningful.

Anaphora

Repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses for emphasis and rhythm.

Antagonist

The force or character in opposition to the protagonist — not necessarily a villain; can be a circumstance, society, or internal conflict.

Chiasmus

Rhetorical figure where the second half mirrors the first in reverse. "Not everything faced can be changed, but nothing can be changed until it is faced." (Baldwin)

Connotation

Emotional or associative meanings a word carries beyond its literal definition. Contrast with denotation.

Denotation

The literal, dictionary definition of a word — stripped of emotional association.

Dramatic Irony

When the audience knows something a character doesn't, creating tension or tragic effect.

Ethos

Rhetorical appeal based on the speaker's credibility or character. One of Aristotle's three modes of persuasion.

Euphemism

A mild expression used instead of a blunt one. Often obscures reality — worth analyzing what is being softened and why.

Fallacy

A flaw in reasoning. Common types: ad hominem, straw man, false dichotomy, slippery slope, hasty generalization.

Foil

A character who contrasts with another to highlight particular traits. Doesn't have to be an antagonist.

Hyperbole

Deliberate exaggeration for effect. Signals emotional intensity — ask what the exaggeration reveals about the speaker.

Imagery

Language appealing to the senses. Effective imagery creates embodied understanding, not just visual pictures.

Intertextuality

The way texts reference, respond to, or build upon other texts. All literature is in conversation with what came before.

Logos

Rhetorical appeal based on logic and evidence. Can be misused through fallacious reasoning or cherry-picked data.

Metaphor

A direct comparison transferring meaning without "like" or "as." What a metaphor includes and excludes both matter.

Motif

A recurring element that develops meaning through repetition. Distinct from theme: a motif is a recurring element; a theme is the idea the work develops.

Pathos

Rhetorical appeal based on emotion or empathy. Not inherently manipulative — connecting to shared experience is legitimate rhetoric.

Literary & Rhetorical Terms — N through Z

Reading · Speaking

Narrator

The voice telling the story. Not the author. First person (I), third limited (one character's perspective), third omniscient (all characters).

Oxymoron

Two contradictory terms combined. Holds a tension a single word cannot.

Personification

Giving human qualities to non-human things. Ask: what kind of person is this thing being compared to? What does that imply?

Protagonist

The central character whose journey drives the narrative. Not necessarily a hero or a morally good person.

Register

The variety of language appropriate to a particular social situation. You code-switch between registers constantly.

Rhetoric

The art of effective communication — how language persuades, informs, and moves audiences. Not a synonym for empty speech.

Satire

The use of humor, irony, or exaggeration to critique human behavior or society. Usually has a serious underlying purpose.

Simile

A comparison using "like" or "as." Unlike metaphor, maintains a clear boundary between the things being compared.

Subtext

What is implied rather than stated — the meaning beneath what characters say or do. Often more significant than the text itself.

Syntax

The arrangement of words and phrases to create sentences. A craft choice — how a sentence is built shapes how it feels and what it means.

Theme

An arguable claim about human experience that a text develops. Not a topic word ("love") but a statement about what the text argues about that topic.

Tone

The author's attitude toward the subject, expressed through language. Distinct from mood (the reader's emotional response).

Warrant

The assumption connecting evidence to a claim in an argument. Often unstated — making warrants explicit reveals whether logic holds.

AR 2023 ELA Standards — What They Mean for You

All Strands · Grades 9–10

These codes appear on rubrics and feedback. Here is what they mean in plain language.

StandardIn Plain Language
Reading: Literary Text
RL.9-10.1I can cite the best available evidence from a literary text and explain what the text implies beyond what it states directly.
RL.9-10.2I can identify what a text argues about human experience (theme) and trace how that argument develops.
RL.9-10.3I can analyze how complex characters develop, interact, and drive plot and theme — especially with conflicting motivations.
RL.9-10.4I can analyze how specific word choices, including figurative language, create cumulative effects on meaning and tone.
RL.9-10.5I can analyze how structural choices — order, pacing, parallel plots — create effects like tension or surprise.
RL.9-10.6I can analyze how a particular cultural experience or perspective shapes what a text can say.
RL.9-10.7I can compare how the same subject is represented across different artistic mediums.
RL.9-10.9I can analyze how an author draws on and transforms source material in their own work.
Reading: Informational Text
RI.9-10.1I can cite strong evidence from informational texts and recognize what they imply beyond what they state directly.
RI.9-10.2I can identify the central idea of an informational text and trace how it develops.
RI.9-10.6I can identify an author's purpose and analyze how they use rhetoric to advance it.
RI.9-10.7I can integrate information from multiple sources, recognizing what each emphasizes and omits.
RI.9-10.8I can evaluate whether an argument's reasoning is valid and its evidence sufficient — and identify fallacies.
RI.9-10.9I can analyze how seminal U.S. documents address enduring themes and questions.
Writing
W.9-10.1I can write an argument with a precise claim, valid reasoning, relevant evidence, and fair acknowledgment of counterarguments.
W.9-10.2I can write explanatory texts that convey complex ideas clearly through organization and precise language.
W.9-10.3I can write narratives using effective technique, well-chosen details, pacing, and meaningful reflection.
W.9-10.5I can develop my writing through planning, revising, editing, and seeking feedback.
W.9-10.9I can draw evidence from literary and informational texts to support my analysis — not just as decoration.
Speaking & Listening
SL.9-10.1I can participate in academic discussion — building on others' ideas, citing evidence, asking for reasoning.
SL.9-10.3I can evaluate a speaker's argument — their reasoning, their evidence, and whether their rhetoric is fair.
SL.9-10.4I can present information clearly, logically, and appropriately for my audience.
Language
L.9-10.1I can use grammatical structures — parallel structure, phrases, clauses — with purpose and control.
L.9-10.3I can make deliberate stylistic choices about syntax and diction — not just avoid errors, but use language consciously.
L.9-10.4I can determine the meaning of unfamiliar words using context, morphology, and reference materials.
L.9-10.5I can understand and use figurative language and nuances in word meaning with precision.
L.9-10.6I can use academic and domain-specific vocabulary accurately in reading, writing, speaking, and listening.
